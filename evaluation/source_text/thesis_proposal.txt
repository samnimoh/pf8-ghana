[normal] Population Genomic Structure and Signatures of Antimalarial Selection in Plasmodium falciparum Across Ghana's Ecological Zones 

[normal] Abstract

[normal] Ghana spans five distinct ecological zones — Sudan savanna, Guinea savanna, transitional zone, moist semi-deciduous forest, and coastal savanna — with substantially different malaria transmission intensities. Existing MalariaGEN partner studies have sampled specific sites (Kassena-Nankana, Cape Coast) but a systematic whole-genome comparison across the entire north-south ecological gradient — leveraging Pf8's expanded sample set — has not been published. This analysis will determine the magnitude of parasite gene flow between zones, test whether major geographic features act as dispersal barriers, and characterise zone-specific alleles relevant to adaptation, drug resistance, and virulence.

[normal] Problem Statement

[normal] Malaria transmission intensity in Ghana is highly heterogeneous, heavily influenced by the country’s diverse geography. Ghana spans five distinct ecological zones along a north-south gradient—the Sudan savanna, Guinea savanna, transitional zone, moist semi-deciduous forest, and coastal savanna—each characterized by unique rainfall patterns, vector distributions, and transmission dynamics. Understanding how Plasmodium falciparum adapts to these varied environments is critical for tailoring localized malaria elimination strategies.

[normal] To date, genomic surveillance efforts in Ghana, including those via the MalariaGEN network, have largely relied on localized sampling from specific indicator sites such as Kassena-Nankana in the north and Cape Coast in the south. While these studies have provided valuable insights, a systematic, whole-genome comparative analysis across the entire north-south ecological gradient has not yet been published. The release of the expanded MalariaGEN Pf8 dataset offers an unprecedented sample size and broader geographic coverage, yet this resource remains unexploited for a comprehensive cross-zonal study in Ghana.

[normal] Consequently, significant knowledge gaps remain regarding the spatial population structure of the parasite. It is currently unknown to what extent parasite gene flow occurs between these ecological zones, or whether major geographic features act as physical barriers to dispersal. Furthermore, potential zone-specific genetic variations linked to local adaptation, drug resistance, and virulence remain uncharacterized. Without this spatial granularity, public health interventions operate under the assumption of a homogenous parasite population, risking the failure of control measures if drug resistance or highly virulent strains migrate undetected across ecological boundaries.

[normal] Main Aim

[normal] The primary aim of this thesis is to conduct a systematic, genome-wide analysis of Plasmodium falciparum isolates across Ghana’s five distinct ecological zones using the expanded MalariaGEN Pf8 dataset to characterize parasite connectivity, geographic barriers to dispersal, and zone-specific adaptive signatures.

[normal] Specific Objectives

[normal] To achieve the main aim, the study will fulfill the following specific objectives:

[normal] To quantify the magnitude and direction of *Plasmodium falciparum* gene flow and population genetic structure across Ghana's five ecological zones.

[normal] To evaluate whether major geographic or ecological transitions along the north-south gradient serve as functional dispersal barriers to parasite migration.

[normal] To identify and characterize zone-specific genomic variants (alleles) associated with local environmental adaptation, antimalarial drug resistance markers, and virulence factors.

[normal] LITERATURE REVIEW

[Heading 2] Global context and burden of malaria

[normal] 1.1 The global malaria burden and changing epidemiology

[normal] The global estimated number of Malaria cases is around 263 million with a 597,000 mortality.The regional breakdown in 2026 also reveals that the WHO African Regions continues to bear approximately 95% of the total global malaria burden.(Alharbi & Ahmed, 2026). In Ghana the disease remains endemic with a high malaria attributable morbidity and mortality,particularly among pregnant women and children under 5 years of age.Historically, however significant strides have been made in the fight against Malaria. The data collectively illustrate a clear pattern of significant decline in Malaria cases from 2000-2015, followed by a plateau and stagnation post 2015.(Alharbi & Ahmed, 2026; Mace et al., 2022).Since 2000, malaria interventions have averted about 2.3billion cases and 14 million deaths, showing that sustained control efforts have a positive impact on case decline.In Ghana an increase in and strengthening of the implementation of interventions such as nationwide coverage of indoor residual spraying(IRS) and promotion of insecticide treated nets and larviciding contributed to the initial drastic reduction in the national prevalence of malaria from 50% in 2012 to 24.4% in 2016(Abukari et al., 2019; Bhatt et al., 2015).Despite substantial progress in scaling up vector control and diagnostic interventions, the stagnation of mortality reduction rates highlights the persistent challenges posed by other important biological and environmental variables. (Amoah et al., 2021).

[normal] Pharmacologically,considering the past decade and change of antimalarials used globally,the movement from chloroquine as the mainstay treatment to Artemisinine Based Combination therapies(ACTs)  contributed to the observed victories chalked in that time period. The ACTs was necessitated by the widespread failure of monotherapies due to the rapid accumulation of resistance-conferring mutations in the parasite genome. This transition toward combination regimens was strategically designed to leverage the distinct mechanisms of action of the partner drugs, thereby delaying the selection of multi-drug resistant strains.(Aregawi et al., 2017).However, the emergence of partial resistance in Southeast Asia and its subsequent spread to other malaria-endemic regions pose a critical threat to the long-term efficacy of these regimens.(Woodrow and White,2017)

[normal] 1.2 The rise of antimalarial drug resistance

[normal] The successive loss of chloroquine and sulfadoxine–pyrimethamine as viable first-line therapies across West Africa is conventionally narrated as a sequence of policy responses to declining clinical efficacy, though the underlying process is more accurately characterised as directional selection acting on parasite populations of substantial effective size. Chloroquine resistance in Ghana, mediated principally by pfcrt K76T with modulation by pfmdr1 polymorphisms, prompted formal withdrawal of the drug from first-line use in 2005 (Abugri et al., 2018). Antifolate resistance followed a comparable trajectory. What distinguishes the antifolate case is persistence: pfdhfr and pfdhps mutations remain at high frequency in Ghanaian isolates despite the drug’s restriction to intermittent preventive treatment in pregnancy, and recent genomic surveillance confirms sustained sulfadoxine–pyrimethamine resistance alongside a substantial reversion toward chloroquine susceptibility (Girgis et al., 2023). Such asymmetry implies that fitness costs differ appreciably between the two resistance phenotypes.

[normal] Selection of this kind leaves detectable genomic signatures, among them reduced nucleotide diversity flanking the selected locus, extended haplotype homozygosity, and allele frequency trajectories inconsistent with drift alone. Amambua-Ngwa et al. (2018), comparing temporally and spatially separated Gambian populations, reported that such signatures are both recoverable from contemporary isolates and discriminating between populations with divergent intervention histories. Whether comparable resolution is attainable in Ghana depends in part on transmission intensity, since elevated entomological inoculation rates sustain high multiplicity of infection, and the resulting outcrossing may erode haplotype structure at a rate approaching that at which selection generates it.

[normal] An additional consideration concerns spatial connectivity. Ghana’s ecological gradient, that is the Sahel savannah in the north, forest through the middle belt, coastal savannah in the south, encompasses appreciable variation in seasonality and vector composition, yet is traversed by sustained internal migration. Microsatellite analysis across these three major zones have indicated limited differentiation among parasite populations, consistent with extensive gene flow (Amoah et al., 2021), although marker-based inference of this kind resolves structure only coarsely. Marker frequencies nonetheless vary regionally , and the tension between these observations has not been settled at genome-wide scale.(Matrevi et al., 2024)

[normal] 1.3 Artemisinin-Based Combination Therapies and Their Central Role in Malaria Control

[normal] The rationale underlying artemisinin-based combination therapy (ACT) rests on a pharmacokinetic asymmetry between components. Artemisinin derivatives achieve rapid reduction of parasite biomass within a single asexual cycle but are cleared from circulation within hours. For this reason the other partner drugs such as lumefantrine, piperaquine or amodiaquine persist with longer durations of action, persist in circulation over days to weeks and eliminate residual parasitaemia. This brief exposure of the artemisinin component was long presumed to contribute to the constraint on the window available for resistance selection by the parasites.

[normal] That assumption has been substantially qualified by observations from East Africa and the Horn. Validated pfkelch13 variants, including R561H in Rwanda (Uwimana et al., 2021), A675V and C469Y in northern Uganda (Balikagala et al., 2021), and R622I in Eritrea, appear to have arisen indigenously rather than through importation from the Greater Mekong Subregion. In the Eritrean case, artemisinin partial resistance was documented in parasites also carrying hrp2/hrp3 deletions, compounding therapeutic and diagnostic failure (Mihreteab et al., 2023). Delayed parasite clearance does not itself constitute treatment failure. Its epidemiological significance lies rather in the enlarged residual parasite burden presented to the partner drug, since it is partner drug resistance that has historically precipitated regimen failure.

[normal] Against this background, the relevant question for Ghana then becomes arguably less about whether pfkelch13 variants are present than whether prevailing population structure would facilitate or impede the spread of an emergent resistant lineage. Nanopore-based surveillance of clinical isolates from Accra and Navrongo detected no evidence of artemisinin resistance (Girgis et al., 2023), and therapeutic efficacy studies continue to report cure rates exceeding WHO thresholds. Such findings are reassuring, with the caveat that surveillance predicated on candidate markers is by construction retrospective, registering emergence only once it has taken place.

[normal] Characterisation of baseline population structure is therefore prerequisite to any early-warning framework.In the absence of baseline data on spatial genetic structure and gene flow, an observed increase in the frequency of a novel haplotype cannot be reliably distinguished from stochastic demographic fluctuations.

[normal] 2. Evolution and Population Genetics of Plasmodium falciparum

[normal] 2.1 Parasite genetic diversity and microevolution

[normal] Few eukaryotic pathogens carry a genome as polymorphic as that of Plasmodium falciparum. When sequencing was done earlier in this millennium , sixteen geographically dispersed isolates, they recovered nearly 47,000 single nucleotide polymorphisms and a nucleotide diversity of roughly 1.16 × 10⁻³, and that variation fell unevenly across functional categories.(Volkman et al., 2006). The intervening two decades have changed the scale of the evidence by orders of magnitude. MalariaGEN's Pf7 release curated genotype calls at six million positions across more than 20,000 samples drawn from 33 countries (MalariaGEN, 2023); its successor Pf8 pushes this to over 33,000 samples and twelve million positions, the largest open variation dataset assembled for any eukaryote till date(MalariaGEN, 2025)

[normal] Mutation supplies novel alleles to the parasite. Its obligate sexual stage in the mosquito midgut is what recombines them into new haplotypes. A vector that takes up gametocytes from a host carrying genetically distinct clones sets meiotic recombination in motion, whereas a vector that takes up a single clone produces what amounts to selfing.(Ouologuem et al., 2023).Recombination frequency therefore scales with the frequency of multiclonal infection, which itself scales with transmission intensity. Kwiatkowski (2024) formalised the relationship in a genomic transmission graph, showing that superinfection, cotransmission and the crossing rate of transmission chains jointly govern effective recombination and coalescent structure. 

[normal] Figure 1. Superinfection leads to cotransmission and recombination of parasites with different ancestral histories.

[normal] One parasite species will behave quite differently at the genomic level in one place than in another because the transmission architecture differs at these different geographic locations.

[normal] Multiplicity of Infection (MOI) in epidemiology refers to the number of separate, independent times a person gets infected with a pathogen during a single disease episode. Pooling 52 studies across 23 sub-Saharan African countries, a mean MOI of 2.09 and a polyclonal infection prevalence of 63%, with regional values running from 1.96 in West Africa to 2.37 in Central Africa was reported. (Mwesigwa et al., 2024). Sondo et al. (2020) saw monthly MOI in Burkina Faso rise steeply in the rainy season and correlate with malaria incidence, with infected patients carrying on average 2.73 distinct genotypes. Closer to the present study, Abukari et al. (2019) compared two Greater Accra sites that sit only 92 km apart but differ in transmission intensity and found correspondingly different diversity and MOI profiles. Ninety-two kilometres is not far but the fact that the gap was still enough to produce measurable differences is an early sign that the spatial scale relevant to P. falciparum population structure in Ghana may be finer than continental analyses would imply.(Abukari et al., 2019)

[normal] Genomic diversity is not spread evenly across the genome, and where there is a deviation from uniformity may be informative of the particular force shaping it. The var multigene family, which encodes the PfEMP1 variant surface antigen, recombines so rapidly that for years it resisted use in surveillance.Deep sequencing of the DBLα domain has recently made it tractable and exposed population structure both within and between continents (Tonkin-Hill et al., 2021). Vaccine candidate antigens carry elevated diversity bearing signatures of balancing selection, consistent with polymorphism maintained by immune pressure (Naung et al., 2022). Drug resistance loci in contrast shows reduced diversity, extended haplotypes, and traces of recent selective sweeps (Volkman et al., 2006;Coulibaly et al., 2022). Such contrasts are the raw material of population genomic inference, since the shape of a locus reports on whatever has been acting upon it.

[normal] In Ghana specifically, Tandoh et al. (2021) analyzed 12,198 genomic loci and found balancing selection at genes that make up the putative genetic background for artemisinin resistance. Their argument is that such signatures might precede, and so give warning of, the selection of kelch13 variants themselves. While prospective validation remains to be established, the claim serves as a critical reminder that resistance evolution is not confined to canonical marker loci, and that the genomic context into which a resistance allele arrives may decide whether it spreads.(Tandoh et al., 2021)

[normal] 2.2 Principles of population genetic structure

[normal] Population structure, in the population-genetic sense, is the non-random distribution of allele frequencies across subpopulations. It is apparent when a single biological species is divided into smaller, distinct sub-groups that do not mate randomly with each other.(Hamilton, 2021)

[normal] Geneticists split total genetic variation into two components: how much diversity sits within a subpopulation, and how much distinguishes one subpopulation from another. Wright's fixation index, F_ST, measures the second component, the share of variance attributable to differences between subpopulations, though it loses resolution once within-population diversity is already high. Three forces sustain that structure: drift, gene flow, and selection. Drift, the random change in allele frequencies from one generation to the next, bites hardest in small populations and pulls subpopulations apart. Gene flow, the movement of alleles through migration, pulls the other way and homogenizes frequencies. Selection can go either direction, depending on whether the same pressures act everywhere or vary by place (Rougeron et al., 2022). Put drift and gene flow together under a neutral model and a familiar pattern emerges.Genetic difference grows with geographic distance, since neighboring populations trade more migrants than distant ones do. That pattern, isolation by distance (IBD), is the default expectation against which real data gets checked.

[normal] It is argued, persuasively, that this neutralist framing has dominated empirical population genetics to its cost. Separating isolation by dispersal limitation, the classical route to IBD, from two alternatives in which local adaptation is what restricts effective gene flow. Under isolation by adaptation, migrants that land in ecologically dissimilar habitats suffer reduced fitness and pass on disproportionately few alleles, so differentiation comes to track environmental dissimilarity rather than distance; the resulting pattern is isolation by environment (IBE). Under monopolization, early colonizers adapt locally and thereby resist later gene flow, entrenching founder effects. On reviewing the empirical record, Orsini and colleagues found patterns attributable to local adaptation to be about as common as those attributable to dispersal limitation alone.(Orsini et al., 2013)

[normal] That distinction matters here. Should parasite populations across Ghana differ merely because they are far apart, the signature to expect is IBD, with differentiation scaling with kilometres and doing so uniformly across the genome. Should they differ instead because ecological zones impose different selective regimes, the signature is IBE, with differentiation scaling with environmental dissimilarity and, importantly, concentrated at the loci under differential selection rather than spread genome-wide. Telling the two apart means comparing patterns at putatively neutral markers with patterns at candidate loci. The marker panel assembled for this study is designed to make exactly that comparison possible.

[normal] Detecting local adaptation in real landscapes is harder than the tidy theory suggests. Forester et al. (2016) used simulations to show that local adaptation strengthens as selective regimes become more spatially aggregated yet survives in patchy landscapes wherever selection is moderate to strong. They also found that univariate genotype–environment association methods threw up false positive rates as high as 55% under limited dispersal, precisely because strong IBD imitates the signal of environmental association. Multivariate, ordination-based approaches are handled that confound far better. The lesson for method is blunt. Spatial and environmental structure are collinear in most real systems, and any analysis that fails to separate them explicitly will credit to one what actually belongs to the other.

[normal] A second theoretical result narrows where we should be looking. Yeaman and Whitlock (2011) showed that migration–selection balance drives adaptation toward concentrated architectures: fewer alleles, larger effects, tighter linkage. Gene flow erodes divergence wherever selection is weak, and small-effect variants are progressively replaced by ones strong enough to resist that erosion.

[normal] Human travel and vector dispersal connect Ghana's parasite population across the country. Drug selection should therefore appear as sharp peaks at a small number of loci rather than a faint signal spread across the genome.

[normal] Sakamoto et al. (2025) arrive at a similar conclusion by another route. Locally adaptive alleles persist much more readily, they show, when environmental variation is spatially autocorrelated, with nearby sites resembling one another and distant ones diverging. Ecological zones stratification meet that condition and are expected to corroborate this phenomenon.

[normal] 2.3 Spatial genetic structure: gene flow, drift, and local adaptation

[normal] For an obligate parasite with a two-host life cycle, the population-genetic framework must be adapted before it fits. Gene flow in P. falciparum is not the parasite dispersing under its own power but a composite of two processes it cannot control: the movement of infected humans and the flight range of infected mosquitoes. Vector dispersal works at the scale of kilometers. Human movement works at the scale of hundreds of kilometers, and it follows roads, trade routes and labor migration rather than diffusing evenly in all directions. The connectivity surface that results is anisotropic and largely uncoupled from Euclidean distance.

[normal] The pattern shows up at continental scale. Amambua-Ngwa et al. (2019) clustered 2,263 isolates from 24 settings in 15 African countries and recovered major western, central and eastern ancestries alongside a divergent Ethiopian population, with the ancestry boundaries tracking historical human migration rather than simple geography. The strongest recent identity-by-descent between populations fell at drug resistance loci: parasites thousands of kilometres apart shared haplotypes at exactly the loci under the heaviest selection. Gene flow, then, is not uniform along the genome. Selection can ferry a haplotype across a continent while the neutral background stays regionally structured.

[normal] Within countries the picture is more varied. Using molecular inversion probes, Verity et al. (2020) sequenced 2,537 infections from the Democratic Republic of the Congo and its neighbours and found an east–west divide in the haplotypes conferring chloroquine and sulfadoxine–pyrimethamine resistance, coexisting with highly related parasites separated by large distances. Gwarinda et al. (2023) documented substantial cross-border connectivity among pre-elimination populations in southern Africa, mapping source and sink populations and blocks of high parasite connectivity. That mapping matters for regional elimination strategy, because a population that is a net importer will never be cleared by local action alone.

[normal] Tanzania supplies an instructive counterpoint. Pereus et al. (2026) sequenced 7,199 samples across ten regions stratified by transmission intensity and recovered F_ST values between regions of 0–0.006, effectively no differentiation at all, together with weak relatedness (pairwise IBD < 0.1) and overlapping population clusters. Complexity of infection tracked transmission intensity closely; spatial genetic structure was all but absent. High transmission inflates effective population size and multiplies outcrossing, and the two together grind down differentiation. For Ghana this carries a caution. In a high-transmission setting, neutral-marker F_ST can be uninformatively small even where selection has driven pronounced differentiation at particular loci, and a study that inspected only genome-wide differentiation might conclude, wrongly, that nothing was happening.

[normal] Which brings us back to the central analytical point. In a highly recombining, high-transmission parasite population, the neutral genome should be close to panmictic over moderate distances. Selection at specific loci is then the process most likely to generate spatial structure that can be detected. Such structure will be locus-specific rather than genome-wide, so the right analysis is not to compute one overall F_ST but to weigh differentiation at candidate loci against a neutral background, asking whether particular loci stand out as outliers relative to what drift and gene flow alone would produce

[normal] 3. Spatial Structure Under Drug and Transmission Pressures

[normal] 3.1 Heterogeneous transmission intensity and its effects on parasite populations

[normal] Transmission intensity in sub-Saharan Africa swings by orders of magnitude over distances of tens of kilometres. Entomological inoculation rates run from fewer than one infective bite per person per year in some coastal and highland settings to several hundred in hyperendemic savannah. Linking a large database of field surveys to reconstructed intervention coverage, Bhatt et al. (2015) estimated that prevalence in endemic Africa halved between 2000 and 2015, with insecticide-treated nets responsible for most of the cases averted. The decline was anything but uniform, though; it was mediated by baseline endemicity, ecology and intervention coverage, each of which varies from place to place. Snow(2015) presses the complementary point from the epidemiological side, insisting that Africa's malaria ecology is diverse enough that no single control strategy can succeed everywhere, and that eradication planning which ignores that diversity will founder on it.

[normal] In principle the genetic consequences of this heterogeneity are well understood. High transmission brings frequent multiclonal infection, and with it frequent outcrossing, high effective recombination, a large effective population size, fast decay of linkage disequilibrium and efficient selection. The same regime purges deleterious variants efficiently and erases drift-induced differentiation quickly. Low transmission inverts all of this: mostly monoclonal infections, effective selfing, extended haplotypes, a small effective population size, and clonal lineages that persist for years.

[normal] Carrasquilla et al. (2022) bring the low-transmission end to life. From 166 genomes off the Pacific coast of Colombia and Ecuador they identified seventeen highly related clonal lineages, six of which had persisted for at least a decade, and yet the population had still adapted repeatedly to changing drug regimes, carrying hard selective sweeps at pfcrt and pfaat1. There is a warning here that is easy to miss. Small, inbred parasite populations are not evolutionarily inert; resistance can and does evolve within them, which is exactly why regions nearing elimination merit more genomic surveillance, not less.

[normal] For this study the more directly relevant work is Mobegi et al. (2014), a genome-wide analysis of selection in two West African populations of contrasting endemicity: 100 isolates from Guinea, where transmission is near-perennial, and 52 from The Gambia, where it is sharply seasonal. Both populations shared a similar set of loci under balancing selection, mostly merozoite-stage genes and validated targets of acquired immunity, which suggests that immune-mediated selection behaves much the same across the region. Signatures of recent directional selection, by contrast, were largely population-specific, and they included differences at drug resistance loci that trace back to historically divergent antimalarial use. The F_ST analysis was the most striking part: exceptional differentiation at a handful of loci, above all a 15 kb region spanning gdv1, a regulator of early gametocyte development. The authors put this down to the extreme difference in vector abundance and seasonality between the two settings, which fixes the transmission opportunity available to sexual-stage parasites.

[normal] That result is the closest published analogue to what is proposed here. Two closely related West African populations, connected by gene flow, still differentiated at specific loci in response to locally varying selection: drug pressure at the resistance loci, transmission seasonality at a gametocytogenesis regulator. The genome-wide background stayed homogeneous. The signal lived entirely in the outliers.

[normal] Whether these genetic metrics can be run in reverse to infer transmission intensity has been tested head-on. Watson et al. (2019) built an individual-based transmission model, evaluated six candidate metrics, and predicted malaria prevalence with a mean absolute error of 0.055, though accuracy leaned heavily on the availability of patient metadata. Working with 1,097 samples from Senegal's national surveillance programme, Schaffner et al. (2023) found the proportion of polygenomic infections to be the best genetic proxy for local incidence, though the relationship frayed at low incidence, while local genetic diversity told them little. What proved diagnostic was the type of relatedness. Two nearby areas with similarly high fractions of related parasites turned out to differ in kind, one dominated by clones and the other by outcrossed relatives, so that different transmission processes had produced superficially similar summary statistics. The caution generalises: summary statistics hide process, and one F_ST or heterozygosity value can be reached from quite different underlying dynamics.

[normal] Ghanaian data fit the broader pattern. Using microsatellite analysis, Amoah et al. (2021) compared P. falciparum populations across three ecological zones and reported zone-associated differences in diversity and population dynamics. Ghansah et al. (2023) set SNP barcoding, microsatellite genotyping and varcoding against one another for their power to detect the impact of an indoor residual spraying intervention in a high-transmission Ghanaian setting, and found multiclonal infection to be the norm and the choice of molecular method to shape the inferences materially. That last finding deserves weight in any study design. A marker panel is not a neutral window onto the population; it is a filter that decides what can be seen at all.

[normal] 3.2 Antimalarial drug use as a spatially varying selection pressure

[normal] The best example of a selective pressure that varies in space is drug pressure, which is a purely non-parasite-driven variation. Treatment-seeking behaviour, services density, diagnostics, prescriptions, the availability of drugs in the private sector and adherence are all trans-administrative and trans-ecological. Two populations of parasites can thus be exposed to quite different drug regimens under the same national flag and they can be exposed to these regimens year after year.

[normal] The classic example is the worldwide history of chloroquine. Independent resistance mutations occurred on a few occasions, were transmitted as selective sweeps and left the extended low-diversity haplotypes that are still discernible in contemporary genomes (Volkman et al., 2006; Cooper et al., 2005). Following the withdrawal of chloroquine, the wild-type allele re-expanded, but at varying frequencies across sites, due to the influence of the transmission intensity and to the presence of the other 4-aminoquinolines with residual activity. In The Gambia, the putative amino acid transporter variant pfaat1 S258L rose from 0% to 97% over the years between 1984 and 2014 following pfcrt K76T, which enhances resistance at a fitness cost, while the other variant prevalent in Southeast Asia did the opposite, reducing resistance and increasing fitness. From this evidence, resistance is an evolutionary process which is more multi-locus than single locus, and the terms of the bargain they negotiate are negotiated locally.

[normal] Chemoprevention lays down a further layer of spatial heterogeneity. Seasonal malaria chemoprevention using amodiaquine plus sulfadoxine–pyrimethamine is used in areas of highly seasonal transmission, but not in areas of perennial transmission, leading to a sharp antifolate gradient across an ecological boundary. In Burkina Faso, before SMC adoption, the pfdhfr triple mutant was 43.6%, compared to 89.4% post SMC adoption and the pfdhfr/pfdhps quintuple mutant was 18.6% before SMC adoption vs 58.3% after SMC adoption (Bohissou et al. 2024). Intermittent PT of pregnancy (SP-based) maintains the pressure of antifolate, even when SP has been dropped for case management. This separation between the pressure on the drug and treatment policy is something that molecular surveillance can detect that clinical surveillance can't.

[normal] Verity et al. (2020) provide the most definitive published evidence of the spread of parasites in space within a single country under the influence of drug pressure. They showed a bi-modal distribution of chloroquine and SP resistance haplotypes in the DRC which could not be accounted for by neutral demography, corresponding to a different history of deployment of the drugs. Even with strong evidence for high levels of gene flow, the allopatric distribution of the highly similar parasites did not change, as was discussed above, even though the neutral genome was well mixed; selection can hold locus-specific differentiation despite high levels of gene flow.

[normal] 3.3 Integrating geography, transmission, and drug pressure in malaria population genetics

[normal] The three strands above meet in a single analytical proposition. Geography sets connectivity, and connectivity sets gene flow. Transmission intensity sets the outcrossing rate and the effective population size, which between them govern how efficiently selection works and how fast drift-induced differentiation is worn away. Drug pressure sets the direction and strength of selection at resistance loci. Each of these varies across space, and, crucially, they vary together, because ecological zone is at once a determinant of vector abundance, of transmission seasonality, of human settlement pattern and of how far the health system reaches.

[normal] This covariance is at once the opportunity and the obstacle. The opportunity, because ecological zone gives a single mappable variable that rolls several selective pressures into one, which makes it a tractable unit of analysis at this scope. The obstacle, because collinear predictors cannot be untangled by observational data alone. If savannah parasites differ from forest parasites, the difference might owe to transmission intensity, to drug pressure, to distance, or to some blend of the three, and no quantity of sampling will settle the attribution completely.

[normal] What can be done is to build the analysis so that the competing hypotheses predict different things. Isolation by distance predicts differentiation that scales with kilometres and is spread genome-wide. Isolation by environment acting through drug selection predicts differentiation concentrated at resistance loci, scaling with ecological dissimilarity rather than distance, and faint or absent at neutral markers. Transmission-mediated differences predict shifts in diversity metrics such as MOI, heterozygosity and linkage disequilibrium, without necessarily generating allele frequency differentiation at the resistance loci. These signatures are separable, and a study that measures neutral and candidate loci alike across zones varying in both distance and ecology is well placed to tell them apart.

[normal] The multivariate caution from Forester et al. (2016) bears on this with full weight, and it is no formality. Ghana's ecological zones are laid out in sequence, coastal savannah in the south, forest through the middle belt, Guinea and Sudan savannah to the north, so that ecological dissimilarity and geographic distance run almost perfectly in parallel along the country's north–south axis. Any analysis that regresses genetic differentiation on ecological zone without controlling for distance will duly find an effect and will be unable to say what produced it. That is a design constraint on this study rather than an incidental caveat, and it ought to shape the sampling frame from the outset.

[normal] 4. Molecular Markers of Antimalarial Drug Resistance

[normal] 4.1 Established resistance determinants: pfcrt, pfmdr1, pfdhfr, pfdhps

[normal] Four loci account for the great majority of validated antimalarial resistance in P. falciparum, and their mechanisms are by now understood at structural resolution. Wicht et al. (2020) provide the synthesis, and the outline that follows tracks theirs.

[normal] PfCRT, the chloroquine resistance transporter, sits in the membrane of the digestive vacuole, the acidic organelle where the parasite degrades host haemoglobin and where chloroquine acts by binding heme and blocking its detoxification. The K76T substitution changes the charge of a central cavity and lets the positively charged drug be shuttled out of the vacuole, away from its target. Kim et al. (2019) resolved the structure of a PfCRT isoform to 3.2 Å by cryo-electron microscopy, showed that resistance-conferring mutations cluster on the helices lining an electronegative central cavity, and confirmed by transport assay that the variant protein effluxes chloroquine in a manner dependent on pH and membrane potential. The wild-type protein does not. Molecular dynamics work has since made the trade-off explicit: the very charge change that admits chloroquine to the binding site disrupts the transporter's native peptide-transport function, and that disruption is the physical basis of the fitness cost accompanying resistance (Tanner et al., 2025).

[normal] That fitness cost explains something of direct relevance to Ghana. Once chloroquine is withdrawn the resistant allele loses its advantage and the wild-type K76 allele re-expands. How fast it re-expands depends on transmission intensity, on residual pressure from related 4-aminoquinolines such as amodiaquine, and on the genomic background, pfaat1 included, where the S258L variant potentiates resistance at a fitness cost and seems to underlie regional differences in resistance dynamics (Amambua-Ngwa et al., 2023). Re-expansion is thus not expected to run at one rate everywhere, and the fact that it has not done so in Ghana is taken up in Section 6.3.

[normal] PfMDR1, encoded on chromosome 5 and likewise resident in the digestive vacuole membrane, tunes susceptibility to a broad range of heme-binding antimalarials through both copy number amplification and point mutation. Its behaviour is counterintuitive enough to be worth stating carefully. Using an oocyte expression system, Shafik et al. (2022) showed that wild-type PfMDR1 transports lumefantrine, mefloquine, dihydroartemisinin, piperaquine, amodiaquine and chloroquine, and that field-derived mutant isoforms differ from the wild type and from one another in transport capacity. The isoforms common in chloroquine-resistant parasites transport chloroquine, lumefantrine and mefloquine less well, the reverse of the relationship seen for PfCRT. What follows is collateral drug sensitivity. Mutations that confer chloroquine resistance at the same time raise susceptibility to lumefantrine and mefloquine, because both kinds of change keep the drugs in the cytosol rather than sequestered in the vacuole, cutting chloroquine's access to its vacuolar target while widening lumefantrine's access to its extra-vacuolar one.

[normal] The practical corollary governs how pfmdr1 haplotypes ought to be read. Sustained artemether–lumefantrine use selects for the N86–184F–D1246 (NFD) haplotype, which is tied to reduced lumefantrine susceptibility and is at the same time the chloroquine-sensitive configuration. A population under heavy AL pressure will therefore look as though it is reverting to chloroquine sensitivity while it is actually building tolerance to its current first-line partner drug. Read pfmdr1 as a chloroquine marker alone and the interpretation flips.

[normal] PfDHFR and PfDHPS underlie resistance to pyrimethamine and sulfadoxine respectively, and are usually read as one combined haplotype because the drugs are given together. Their mutations accumulate stepwise and largely additively. In pfdhfr the N51I/C59R/S108N triple mutant is now effectively fixed across much of Africa, with little sign of further accumulation, while the I164L substitution that confers high-level antifolate resistance stays rare in West Africa (White et al., 2025). In pfdhps the picture is more varied: A437G is widespread, K540E is common in East Africa but scarce in the west, and A581G and A613S turn up at low frequency. The pfdhfr triple with pfdhps A437G is conventionally called the quadruple mutant and confers partial resistance; add K540E to reach the quintuple and full resistance; add further substitutions to reach sextuple and higher haplotypes.

[normal] Analysing Pf7 data from 22 African countries, White et al. (2025) found pfdhfr to be limited in haplotype diversity while pfdhps varied widely in both composition and spatial distribution. That asymmetry is useful, since it implies pfdhps carries more spatial information than pfdhfr does. Guémas et al. (2023) documented the recent emergence of the pfdhps vagKgs allele, which incorporates the novel I431V substitution, at high prevalence in Cameroon and Nigeria, their genomic analysis pointing to recent African emergence and clonal expansion. Surveying West African isolates from 2012–2022, Zhou et al. (2024) put I431V at 11.7% overall, with the highest frequency in Nigeria. Whether the allele has reached Ghana, and whether its distribution respects ecological boundaries, is an open question that this study's marker panel is built to address.

[normal] 4.2 kelch13 and the molecular basis of artemisinin resistance

[normal] Artemisinin resistance differs from resistance to the older antimalarials in a way that has bred lasting terminological confusion, and the distinction is not pedantry. Parasites carrying kelch13 propeller mutations are not resistant in the usual sense of surviving therapeutic drug concentrations. Their reduced susceptibility is confined to the early ring stage and shows clinically as delayed parasite clearance rather than outright treatment failure. The World Health Organization labels this artemisinin partial resistance (ART-R). Because artemisinins have a short half-life and always travel with a longer-acting partner drug, partial resistance does not by itself cause ACT failure. What it does is expose a larger residual parasite biomass to partner drug monotherapy, which speeds selection for partner drug resistance. The catastrophic ACT failures documented in the Greater Mekong Subregion came when partial resistance to artemisinin met resistance to the partner (Rosenthal et al., 2024a).

[normal] The mechanism turns on hemoglobin endocytosis. K13 helps take up host hemoglobin into the digestive vacuole, and because artemisinin must be activated by the free heme released as hemoglobin is degraded, less endocytosis in ring-stage parasites means less drug activation and so less drug action. Propeller-domain mutations impair that function, and the phenotype they produce is stage-specific, which accounts both for the ring-stage-restricted survival and for the delayed-clearance presentation (Wicht et al., 2020).

[normal] Not all kelch13 mutations are equal, and the field's classification reflects that. The WWARN K13 Study Group (2019) pooled individual patient data from 3,250 patients across eighteen studies and linked twenty propeller-region alleles to the slow-clearance phenotype, fifteen of them not previously confirmed. Reviewing African K13 frequencies from 2012 to 2020, Ndwiga et al. (2021) found validated mutations only sporadically, at low frequency, and, Rwanda aside, without associated clinical failure. That assessment was accurate the day it was written. It is now out of date, and how quickly it dated is itself part of the case for surveillance.

[normal] Between 2020 and 2023 the African picture changed decisively. Uwimana et al. (2020) genotyped pretreatment samples from Rwandan efficacy trials and found R561H in 7.4% of patients at Masaka; phylogenetics revealed the expansion of an indigenous lineage, and gene editing confirmed that the mutation drives artemisinin resistance in vitro. This was de novo African emergence, not importation from Asia. Balikagala et al. (2021) then supplied the clinical confirmation that had been missing. In northern Uganda, fourteen of 240 patients treated with intravenous artesunate had parasite clearance half-lives beyond five hours, and thirteen of those fourteen carried A675V or C469Y. Ex vivo ring-stage survival assays backed the phenotype, and the prevalence of kelch13 mutations climbed from 3.9% in 2015 to 19.8% in 2019. Flanking single nucleotide polymorphisms differed markedly from the Southeast Asian ones, confirming independent local origin.

[normal] Conrad et al. (2023) then charted the trajectory across Uganda through annual surveillance at ten to sixteen sites from 2016 to 2022. By 2021–22, parasites carrying validated or candidate markers passed 20% prevalence in eleven of the sixteen districts. The 469Y and 675V mutations, first seen in the far north in 2016–17, spread until their combined prevalence reached 10–54% across much of northern Uganda; 561H, once a Rwandan signature, surfaced in southwestern Uganda in 2021 and hit 23% by 2022. Their observation about where emergence happened is worth carrying over in substance: resistance appeared mainly where effective malaria control had lapsed or where transmission was unstable. Awor et al. (2024) used haplotype network analysis to show the same indigenous origin for C469Y, and picked it up more often after rectal artesunate was introduced, evidence of selection under one specific deployment practice.

[normal] Emergence is not confined to the Great Lakes. Fola et al. (2023) found the candidate marker 622I in 8.0% of Ethiopian cases, with identity-by-descent analysis pointing to recent expansion, and, of real operational concern, 8.2% of 622I parasites also carried pfhrp2/3 deletions, stacking drug and diagnostic resistance in one organism. Drawing on pooled deep sequencing across 5,465 samples from Rwanda, Uganda, Tanzania and the DRC, Young et al. (2024) found 561H at 90% of Rwandan sites at a mean frequency of 19.0%, and detected candidate mutations 441L, 449A and 469F co-occurring with validated ones, a sign that they are arising under the same selective pressure.

[normal] How much of this matters for West Africa comes down to whether these alleles can spread. Stokes et al. (2021) tackled the question by editing K13 mutations into a panel of African and Asian strains and measuring both resistance and fitness. Their results are the single most important input to any risk assessment for Ghana. C580Y and M579I conferred elevated resistance but carried a substantial fitness cost, which the authors suggest may slow their spread in high-transmission settings where clones compete intensely within hosts. R561H was different: fitness-neutral in African 3D7 parasites yet conferring resistance on a par with C580Y. A fitness-neutral resistance allele in a high-transmission population meets no counterweight to its spread. That R561H is precisely the allele that emerged in Rwanda and has since crossed into Uganda is unlikely to be coincidence.

[normal] Rosenthal et al. (2024b) lay out the response priorities, the first of which is to expand genomic surveillance across the continent, with pointed emphasis on West and Central Africa, where systematic data remain sparsest. Kojom Foko and Sharma make the same case for the region in particular, noting that emerging ART-R signals, recent cuts in donor funding and armed conflict disrupting control programmes across parts of Central and West Africa together create conditions in which resistance could take hold before anyone detects it. The World Malaria Report 2025 states the convergence starkly, drawing the parallel to the years before chloroquine failure swept the continent while flagging one material difference: molecular surveillance can now catch resistance ahead of clinical failure (Platon et al., 2026). Whether that capacity is deployed in time is a policy question, not a scientific one.

[normal] 4.3 From resistance to tolerance: emerging signals and phenotypic correlates

[normal] The mapping between kelch13 genotype and artemisinin phenotype is imperfect in both directions, and each failure carries a lesson for surveillance design.

[normal] In one direction, kelch13 mutations occur with no resistance phenotype to show for them. Eboumbou Moukoko et al. (2019) found non-synonymous K13 mutations in 2.9% of Cameroonian isolates, none of them tied to delayed clearance. The WWARN meta-analysis likewise found no association between propeller mutations and clearance half-life in African isolates, against the strong association seen in Asia. Acquired immunity plausibly explains the gap, since it clears parasites independently of drug action and so masks the phenotype in semi-immune African patients (WWARN K13 Study Group, 2019). A study leaning on clearance data alone from an immune population would, on that account, underestimate resistance.

[normal] In the other direction, and this is the more troubling one, the resistance phenotype occurs without any kelch13 mutation. Das et al. (2021) documented ACT failure in central India, with prolonged clearance half-lives and reduced ring-stage survival in parasites bearing no pfkelch13 propeller mutation, and concluded that kelch13-independent artemisinin resistance is real. Yu et al. (2025) offered a candidate mechanism. Screening putative histone acetyltransferases across 73 field isolates, they identified PfMYST as fully associated with the modulation of ring-stage survival; knockdown confirmed the link, and multi-omics analysis indicated crosstalk between the PfMYST and PfK13 pathways. This epigenetic modulation of ring-stage survival reached amodiaquine, pyrimethamine, chloroquine and pyronaridine as well. Duffy et al. (2022) reported a related result, showing that dihydroartemisinin tolerance in isolates with naturally acquired K13 mutations, though not in genetically manipulated laboratory strains, was modulated by prior hyperoxic stress, which implicates the genetic background rather than the K13 substitution on its own.

[normal] The implication for a marker-based surveillance study is uncomfortable, and it is better stated than glossed over. A kelch13-negative result does not establish artemisinin susceptibility. It establishes the absence of the currently validated markers, which is a narrower claim. Here is one reason to take seriously the balancing-selection signal that Tandoh et al. (2021) detected at artemisinin-resistance background genes in Ghanaian parasites: if resistance can arise on a permissive genomic background before any canonical marker appears, then watching the background may give earlier warning than watching the marker.

[normal] Phenotypic assays remain the reference standard, and their limits bound what any genotype-only study can infer. The ring-stage survival assay measures the survival of 0–3 hour parasites after a six-hour dihydroartemisinin pulse, taking survival above 1% as a marker of partial resistance. It is laborious, low-throughput and dependent on a microscopy readout that carries its own variance. Davis et al. (2020) built the extended recovery RSA, swapping microscopy for quantitative PCR and stretching incubation to 114 hours, and reported a Spearman correlation of −0.84 with patient clearance half-life, the strongest to that point. Okitwi et al. (2024) validated the method on 122 fresh Ugandan field isolates and found strong correlation with conventional RSA together with modest but significant associations with C469Y and A675V carriage. Intharabut et al. (2019) added an important interpretive caveat. The age distribution of circulating parasites at admission substantially affects apparent clearance, and the effect is stronger in kelch13-mutant infections, so clearance half-life has to be read with reference to parasite stage rather than taken at face value.

[normal] Beyond kelch13, several loci earn a place in a contemporary panel. Pfcoronin has been linked to artemether–lumefantrine failure in malaria imported from Africa; Kegya et al. (2025) found the P76S variant in 29.7% of Kumasi isolates in 2023, a striking prevalence for a marker whose functional significance is still only partly worked out. Pfaat1 S258L, discussed above in connection with chloroquine, sat at 64.7% in the same Kumasi sample. Neither is a WHO-validated marker at present. Both are plausibly informative, and a panel restricted to validated markers alone would have missed the pair of them.

[normal] 5. Spatial Population Genetics in Malaria and Related Systems

[normal] 5.1 Spatial genetic studies of malaria parasites in endemic regions

[normal] Applications of spatial population genetics to P. falciparum have settled on a consistent methodological repertoire, and a review of what has been done clarifies both the precedents this study can draw on and the pitfalls it should sidestep.

[normal] At continental scale, Amambua-Ngwa et al. (2019) set the baseline: three major African ancestries plus a divergent Ethiopian population, with the strongest recent identity-by-descent between populations falling at drug resistance loci. The methodological lesson tucked into that finding is that IBD-based approaches pick up selection-driven connectivity which F_ST-based approaches, averaging over the genome, tend to blur out. Carrasquilla et al. (2022) made the same point outright, showing that IBD analysis resolved the inbred structure of a South American population in far more detail than principal components analysis, ADMIXTURE or distance-based trees, and that it revealed hard selective sweeps at pfcrt and pfaat1 alongside softer, incomplete sweeps at pfdhps, pfmdr1 and pfdhfr.

[normal] At national scale, the DRC study of Verity et al. (2020) is still the most complete demonstration of drug-driven spatial structure within a single country: an east–west divide in chloroquine and SP resistance haplotypes, sitting alongside evidence of extensive gene flow. Gwarinda et al. (2023) came at the problem from the elimination angle, using microsatellite data from four southern African countries to pick out source and sink populations and cross-border connectivity blocks. Pereus et al. (2026) supply the cautionary counterexample discussed earlier, near-zero F_ST across ten Tanzanian regions in spite of pronounced variation in transmission intensity, which shows that an absence of neutral-marker differentiation must not be mistaken for an absence of population structure at loci under selection.

[normal] Miotto et al. (2024) show what fine-grained analysis of Pf7 data can recover. Across 3,783 African genomes they identified a strongly differentiated cluster, AF1, making up 1.2% of samples spread over thirteen countries at points more than 7,000 km apart, sharing a complex genetic background of up to 23 loci while outbreeding with sympatric parasites everywhere outside those loci. For such a configuration the authors propose the term cryptotype: a common genomic background threaded with regions of local origin. That a coherent, continentally distributed genetic entity could go undetected until a dataset of this size existed is a fair argument for the value of large-scale genomic surveillance, and a fair caution against assuming that our current descriptions of population structure are complete.

[normal] Within Ghana the literature is real but thin. Abukari et al. (2019) compared high- and low-transmission sites in Greater Accra using twelve microsatellite markers plus the Msp2 marker, recovering differences in diversity, MOI and population structure over 92 km. Amoah et al. (2021) carried the approach to three ecological zones. Ghansah et al. (2023) compared molecular surveillance methods in a high-transmission setting under an IRS intervention. Tandoh et al. (2021) analysed genome-wide signatures of balancing selection at artemisinin-resistance background genes. What no one has yet done, and this is the gap the present study addresses, is a spatially explicit analysis that treats ecological zone as the structuring variable, samples the full zonal gradient, and sets differentiation at drug resistance loci against a neutral-marker background.

[normal] 5.2 Landscape and spatial genetics of malaria vectors: lessons for the parasite

[normal] The vector literature repays attention for reasons that go past disciplinary courtesy. Anopheles has been worked over with landscape-genetic methods for longer and in more depth than the parasite has, and, decisively for the present argument, the single most relevant published result comes from Ghana.

[normal] Yawson et al. (2007) sampled Anopheles gambiae sensu stricto across Ghana and southern Burkina Faso, in areas where the M and S molecular forms occurred in both sympatry and allopatry, and genotyped microsatellite loci. Neither Bayesian clustering nor F_ST-based analysis turned up differentiation between the molecular forms. What both did turn up was strong differentiation among ecological zones, regardless of M/S status, and, the detail that matters most, no detectable effect of geographic distance. Ecological barriers restricted gene flow; distance and taxonomic status did not. The title puts the conclusion plainly: ecological zones rather than molecular forms predict genetic differentiation.

[normal] This is a structural precedent for the present study's hypothesis, in the same country, in the other organism of the same transmission system. If ecological zone structures the vector's population genetics in Ghana while distance does not, then the possibility that it also structures the parasite's is not speculation but the natural next question. The parallel is not proof. Parasite and vector differ in generation time, effective population size and mode of dispersal, and the parasite's gene flow runs substantially through human movement, which answers to roads rather than biomes. Still, the vector result establishes that Ghana's ecological zones are ecologically consequential boundaries and not merely cartographic conveniences, and establishing that is no small thing.

[normal] The wider vector literature supports the generality of the pattern while sharpening its limits. Pinto et al. (2013) genotyped 25 An. gambiae samples from twelve African countries and found population substructure coincident with the central African rainforest belt and the northern and southern savannah biomes, laying restricted gene flow at the door of the forest–savannah transition. Hemming-Schroeder et al. (2020) applied an explicit landscape-genetics framework across Kenya and identified tree cover as the dominant ecological driver of gene flow in An. gambiae and cropland in An. arabiensis. Different drivers for closely related species is a useful warning against assuming that ecological effects carry over even between congeners. Sequencing 765 genomes for the Ag1000G project, Miles et al. (2017) found complex structure and gene flow patterns together with strong selection signals at insecticide-resistance genes, the sweeps spreading over large distances and even between species. Lehmann et al. (2003) had drawn the continental picture earlier, identifying a major division separating northwestern from southeastern populations, partly along the inhospitable stretches of the Rift Valley.

[normal] Two lessons carry straight over to the parasite study. First, resistance loci behave differently from neutral loci in spatial analyses: insecticide-resistance sweeps in Anopheles crossed species boundaries and continental distances while the neutral background stayed regionally structured, exactly as drug-resistance haplotypes have done in P. falciparum (Amambua-Ngwa et al., 2019; Miles et al., 2017). Selection builds connectivity that neutral demography does not. Second, the vector work shows that ecological covariates can be modelled explicitly rather than treated as a categorical afterthought. Hemming-Schroeder and colleagues identified specific landscape variables, not just zone labels, and so produced a mechanistic account rather than a descriptive one. A parasite study that stratified by zone and stopped there would be leaving that resolution unclaimed.

[normal] 6. Malaria Transmission and Drug Resistance in Ghana

[normal] 6.1 Ecological and transmission zones of Ghana

[normal] Ghana is conventionally split into three or four ecological zones running along a south–north gradient: the coastal savannah, the forest belt of the middle and southwestern regions, and the Guinea and Sudan savannah of the north. Rainfall drives the gradient, bimodal and heavy in the forest zone, unimodal and progressively shorter as one moves north, and it in turn sets vegetation, the availability of vector breeding habitat, and transmission seasonality. Transmission is perennial in the forest zone and markedly seasonal in the northern savannah, with entomological inoculation rates that differ substantially both in magnitude and in how they are distributed through the year.

[normal] The malaria literature has treated Ghana as a zonally structured system for some time, and studies that stratify by zone have consistently recovered zone-associated differences. Abuaku et al. (2012) built therapeutic efficacy surveillance around the three zones. Amoah et al. (2021) sampled parasite populations from three ecological zones and reported zone-associated differences in diversity and dynamics. Yawson et al. (2007), as already noted, found ecological zone to be the dominant predictor of vector population genetic structure. The zonal framework is therefore no imposition of this study but an established organising principle of Ghanaian malaria research, which is convenient, because it makes the sampling frame defensible on prior grounds.

[normal] The constraint flagged in Section 3.3 still holds, and it belongs here in the open rather than buried. Because Ghana's zones are arranged latitudinally, ecological dissimilarity and geographic distance are strongly collinear along the north–south axis. Coastal savannah and Sudan savannah are ecologically more alike than either is to the forest between them, yet they lie maximally apart in space. Handled carefully, that configuration offers a little analytical leverage against the collinearity, because a purely distance-driven process predicts maximal differentiation between the two while a purely ecology-driven process does not.

[normal] 6.2 History of antimalarial policy and artemisinin-based therapy in Ghana

[normal] Ghana replaced chloroquine with artesunate–amodiaquine as first-line therapy for uncomplicated malaria in 2005, following WHO recommendations issued in 2004. Studying drug utilisation in urban Accra after the change, Dodoo et al. (2009) found that although 90.8% of patients received a regimen containing an artemisinin derivative, 33% received an artemisinin monotherapy. That is a substantial gap between policy and practice, and one with obvious consequences for the selective pressure parasite populations actually experience. Prescribing was predicted by laboratory confirmation of diagnosis, patient age, and whether the facility was government-run.

[normal] In 2008 artemether–lumefantrine and dihydroartemisinin–piperaquine joined AS-AQ as first-line options, and artesunate–pyronaridine arrived in 2022, with DHAP subsequently designated second-line (Abuaku et al., 2026). Ghana thus runs a multiple-first-line policy, which means parasite populations in different parts of the country may meet different partner-drug pressure depending on procurement, facility stocking and prescriber preference. This is not a minor administrative wrinkle. It is a mechanism by which drug pressure can vary spatially inside a single national policy framework, and it is hard to observe by any route other than the molecular signature it leaves in the parasite population.

[normal] Therapeutic efficacy has been tracked through sentinel-site surveillance since 2005, and in aggregate the results reassure. Abuaku et al. (2016) reported day-28 PCR-corrected cure rates of 100% for AS-AQ and 97.6% for AL across forest and savannah sites. Covering ten sentinel sites from 2015 to 2017, Abuaku et al. (2019) reported national cure rates of 99.2% for AS-AQ and 96.0% for AL, with day-3 parasitaemia all but absent. The 2023 round found day-42 PCR-corrected cure rates of 100% for DHAP across every site and 97.3% for AP (Abuaku et al., 2026). By WHO criteria, ACT efficacy in Ghana remains high.

[normal] One earlier result complicates that reassurance, and it is the empirical foundation of this study. Abuaku et al. (2012) monitored AL efficacy across four sentinel sites representing the three ecological zones and found day-28 PCR-corrected cure rates that differed significantly by zone: 90.4% in the savannah, 93.8% in the coastal zone, and 100% in the forest. Fever and parasite clearance were both slower in the savannah. Their conclusion is worth restating in their own framing: the savannah zone may be a potential zone for any emergence of resistant alleles as a result of the slower parasite clearance observed there.

[normal] That was written in 2012. No spatially explicit population genomic analysis has followed it up since.

[normal] Effectiveness under unsupervised conditions, which is how ACT is actually taken, tells a somewhat different story from efficacy under directly observed therapy. Mawuli et al. (2024) followed 84 patients treated with AL without supervision at a low-transmission site in Accra and a high-transmission site in Kumbungu, in northern Ghana. In the north, 3.6% were parasitaemic at day 3 and the 28-day PCR-corrected effectiveness came to 89.5%, marginally under the WHO 90% threshold, with every recurrence genotyped as recrudescence rather than reinfection. In Accra no patient was parasitaemic at day 3 and none recurred. Adherence is plausibly part of the story; Chatio et al. (2015) found that in northern Ghana adherence was shaped by education, by perceived cure after the first dose, and by experience of side-effects. Sub-therapeutic exposure from incomplete adherence is exactly the condition under which partial resistance gets selected.

[normal] 6.3 Existing evidence on spatial distribution of resistance markers in Ghanaian P. falciparum

[normal] Molecular surveillance in Ghana has been carried out at multiple sites by several groups, and read together rather than one at a time, the studies already hold evidence of zonal differentiation. They have simply never been analysed as a single spatial dataset.

[normal] The pivotal study is Mensah et al. (2020). Using molecular inversion probes to genotype pfcrt, pfmdr1, pfdhps, pfdhfr and pfk13 in 803 samples collected from 2014 to 2017 at Begoro in the forest zone and Cape Coast on the coast, they found that thirteen years after chloroquine withdrawal the chloroquine-sensitive pfcrt K76 allele had reached 95% prevalence in Begoro but only 71% in Cape Coast, a highly significant difference (Z = −3.5, P < 0.001). Two sites, one country, the same national drug policy, and a 24-percentage-point gap in the frequency of a resistance-associated allele. They also found pfmdr1 184F under strong selection, the pfdhfr/pfdhps quadruple genotype near saturation, and pfdhps 581G at 2–10%, a marker of sulfadoxine resistance that correlates with IPTp failure and had not previously been reported in Ghana.

[normal] Independent studies bear the picture out while adding loci. Dakorah et al. (2025) sequenced 409 samples from five districts of the Central Region and found pfcrt CVIET at only 0.5% against 97% wild-type CVMNK, pfmdr1 NFD at 44%, and pfdhfr IRNI with pfdhps A437G, which together confer partial SP resistance, in 88% of parasites, the full-resistance haplotype adding K540E in a further 9%. Not a single wild-type pfdhfr/pfdhps haplotype turned up. Screening 771 blood donors across five districts of Greater Accra, Aninagyei et al. (2020) found pfcrt CVIET at 11.6% and detected five non-synonymous kelch13 alleles, among them C580Y, the canonical Southeast Asian resistance mutation, at 3.6%, alongside P615L, A578S, I543V and A676S. Culturing 29 isolates from travellers returning from Ghana, Zhao et al. (2022) found no pfk13 propeller mutations, IC50 values below resistance thresholds for every drug except chloroquine and pyrimethamine, and a predominant pfmdr1 86N-184F-1246D haplotype that they read as the signature of artemether–lumefantrine selection.

[normal] The most recent study, and the one closest to this project's location, is Kegya et al. (2025), who genotyped 200 isolates collected in Kumasi in 2023. They found non-synonymous pfk13 mutations in 2.2% of isolates, including the candidate ART-R marker P527H together with S522C, C532S and I590V. Pfcoronin P76S, tied to AL failure in imported African malaria, stood at 29.7%. Pfaat1 S258L stood at 64.7%. The pfmdr1 NFD pattern predominated, consistent with impaired lumefantrine sensitivity. None of these markers was associated with day-3 parasite positivity, and the authors conclude that ACT efficacy in Ghana remains high, but that isolated critical pfk13 polymorphisms are now present against a background of pfmdr1 signals for reduced lumefantrine sensitivity.

[normal] Read as a body rather than one at a time, these studies make three things stand out. First, resistance-marker frequencies in Ghana differ between sites: shown for pfcrt K76 between forest and coast (Mensah et al., 2020), and visible in the differing pfcrt CVIET frequencies between the Central Region (0.5%) and Greater Accra (11.6%) (Dakorah et al., 2025; Aninagyei et al., 2020), though that second comparison confounds site with study design and sample type. Second, kelch13 variants are present but sporadic, and they include both the canonical C580Y and novel candidates such as P527H. Third, no study has yet sampled systematically across the full ecological gradient with a consistent marker panel and analysed the result as a spatial dataset. The evidence for zonal differentiation is already there. It has accumulated as a by-product of studies designed to answer other questions.

[normal] 7. Conceptual Model and Research Gap

[normal] 7.1 Conceptual framework: spatial genetic structure under artemisinin selection

[normal] The framework this study adopts can be set out as a causal chain, and setting it out explicitly clarifies what is being claimed and what is merely being assumed.

[normal] Ecological zone sets vector abundance, breeding habitat and transmission seasonality, and through them the local entomological inoculation rate. EIR sets the frequency of multiclonal infection, and so the outcrossing rate, and so effective recombination and effective population size. At the same time, ecological zone correlates with how far the health system reaches, with treatment-seeking behaviour, with adherence, and, through procurement and prescribing variation under a multiple-first-line policy, with the specific ACT regimens local parasites meet. Drug exposure sets the direction and intensity of selection at the resistance loci. Taken together, these processes leave parasite populations in different zones facing different selective regimes against different demographic backgrounds, and the expected consequence is spatial differentiation in resistance-marker frequencies beyond anything neutral demography alone would produce.

[normal] Three predictions follow, and they can be told apart. Should gene flow be the dominant process, differentiation ought to scale with geographic distance, sit across the whole genome, and appear at neutral and candidate loci alike. Should transmission heterogeneity dominate, diversity metrics such as MOI, expected heterozygosity and linkage disequilibrium ought to differ by zone with no matching allele frequency differentiation at the resistance loci. Should drug selection dominate, differentiation ought to concentrate at the resistance loci, follow ecological zone more closely than distance, and fade or vanish at neutral markers. The published Ghanaian evidence fits the third prediction best, yet it has never been tested against the other two, because no study has measured neutral and candidate loci together across the zonal gradient within one sampling frame.

[normal] The framework carries an operational implication that gives the study a point beyond description. If resistance markers are spatially structured, then national-level marker frequencies, the numbers reported to WHO and used to steer policy, are averages over heterogeneous subpopulations, and they will systematically understate the frequency in the zone where it runs highest. A validated kelch13 mutation at 3% nationally might sit at 12% in the northern savannah and be absent everywhere else, and the national figure would trigger no policy response at all. Spatial resolution is not an academic refinement of surveillance. It is a precondition of surveillance doing the job it exists to do.

[normal] 7.2 Gaps in current knowledge on geographic differentiation and emerging artemisinin tolerance in Ghana

[normal] This study addresses four gaps, and they are not of equal size.

[normal] The first is the absence of spatially explicit analysis. Ghanaian molecular surveillance has been site-based, not gradient-based. Mensah et al. (2020) compared two sites; Dakorah et al. (2025) sampled five districts inside one region; Kegya et al. (2025) sampled a single city. No study has yet sampled the full coastal–forest–savannah gradient with a consistent panel and analysed differentiation as a joint function of zone and distance. The between-site differences observed so far cannot presently be pinned on ecology, on distance, or on differences in sampling and assay methodology between studies. Only one coherent sampling frame can prise these apart.

[normal] The second is the neglect of the savannah signal. Abuaku et al. (2012) named the savannah zone as the probable site of resistant-allele emergence on the strength of slower parasite clearance and a significantly lower cure rate. Mawuli et al. (2024) later found day-3 parasitaemia and sub-threshold effectiveness at a northern site and neither at a southern one. Two independent phenotypic signals, twelve years apart, both point at the same zone. And yet the most recent and most comprehensive molecular studies (Kegya et al., 2025; Dakorah et al., 2025) sampled Kumasi and the Central Region, the forest and coastal zones. The zone flagged as highest-risk is the one characterised least well at the molecular level.

[normal] The third concerns emerging markers. Ghanaian surveillance has centred on validated loci. But the Ugandan and Rwandan emergences were caught because surveillance took in candidate mutations as well as validated ones, and the alleles that ended up mattering, 469Y, 675V and 561H, were all candidates before they were validated (Uwimana et al., 2020; Balikagala et al., 2021; Conrad et al., 2023). Kegya et al. (2025) found P527H, pfcoronin P76S at 29.7%, and pfaat1 S258L at 64.7% in Kumasi. Whether any of these is zone-structured is unknown, because each has been measured at a single site. A panel that carries candidate and background loci across the gradient would settle the question.

[normal] The fourth is the fitness question, which governs what any observed marker frequency actually means. Stokes et al. (2021) showed that C580Y and M579I carry substantial fitness costs whereas R561H is fitness-neutral in an African genomic background. In a high-transmission population with fierce within-host competition, a costly resistance allele may be found at low frequency and stay there indefinitely, while a fitness-neutral one may be found at that same low frequency and be climbing toward fixation. The C580Y found at 3.6% in Ghanaian blood donors (Aninagyei et al., 2020) and the P527H found at low frequency in Kumasi (Kegya et al., 2025) therefore mean quite different things, and a frequency estimate on its own cannot separate them. What can partly separate them is spatial and temporal pattern, since an allele that is spreading leaves a different geographic footprint from one that is being generated and purged over and over.

[normal] 7.3 Rationale for focusing on established markers and emerging kelch13-linked signals

[normal] The marker panel is built to serve three functions, and the rationale behind each one differs.

[normal] The established markers, pfcrt, pfmdr1, pfdhfr and pfdhps, serve as the calibration set. Their resistance phenotypes are mechanistically characterised (Wicht et al., 2020; Kim et al., 2019; Shafik et al., 2022), their African frequencies are documented (White et al., 2025; Zhou et al., 2024), and their Ghanaian baselines already exist (Mensah et al., 2020; Dakorah et al., 2025). Having weathered decades of drug pressure that varied over time and across space, they carry the accumulated spatial signal of that pressure, which makes them the loci most likely to expose zonal structure if any exists. The pfcrt K76 re-expansion gradient between Begoro and Cape Coast shows that the signal is detectable in Ghana with the methods already to hand. The pfmdr1 NFD haplotype, for its part, indexes contemporary artemether–lumefantrine pressure rather than historical chloroquine pressure, so it reads current selection rather than legacy selection.

[normal] Kelch13 serves as the sentinel. Validated mutations remain rare in West Africa, and the plausible outcome here is that few or none are detected. That would not be a negative result. Sentinel surveillance exists to fix a baseline against which future change can be measured, and to catch emergence early enough for a response to be possible, which is the very capability the World Malaria Report 2025 names as the material difference between today and the years before chloroquine failure (Platon et al., 2026). A spatially resolved baseline is strictly more informative than a national one, for the reason set out in Section 7.1: it tells you where to look next.

[normal] The emerging and background loci serve as a hedge against the panel's own assumptions. Kelch13-independent artemisinin resistance exists (Das et al., 2021), epigenetic modulation of ring-stage survival has been shown (Yu et al., 2025), the K13 genomic background modulates the phenotype (Duffy et al., 2022; Stokes et al., 2021), and Ghanaian parasites already display balancing selection at ART-R background genes (Tandoh et al., 2021). A panel confined to validated markers would be blind to all of it. Adding pfcoronin, pfaat1 and the background loci flagged by Tandoh and colleagues does not guarantee a signal will be found. It guarantees that if one is present, the study can see it, which is a lower bar and the right one.

[normal] The design is thus not merely a survey of marker frequencies. It is an attempt to read, from the spatial distribution of those frequencies, the selective history that produced them, and to settle whether the zone that two independent phenotypic studies have flagged as highest-risk really is genetically distinct.
