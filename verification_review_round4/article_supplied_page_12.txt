The reconstructed genotypes were compared against MalariaGEN's published per-sample resistance genotypes 
(MalariaGEN 2025). All 47,012 of 47,012 comparable calls agreed. That comparison covers 25 of the 57 panel 
codons, namely those for which MalariaGEN publishes a per-sample genotype; the remaining 32 have no 
external per-sample check. Every pfk13 codon falls in the unchecked set, so the artemisinin result rests on the 
codon reconstruction itself and on its concordance at the other genes, not on a direct external comparison at 
pfk13.
Discussion
Interpreting very low differentiation
The largest pairwise Hudson FST among Ghana's agro-ecological zones was 0.00232, between coastal savanna 
and Guinea savanna, with a block-jackknife 95% confidence interval of 0.00128 to 0.00336 (Table 2; Hudson et 
al. 1992). This number carries a narrow meaning: it measures differentiation among the four sampled groups at 
the 3,916 common variants retained after filtering, as a ratio of averages over those variants. Five of the six 
intervals exclude zero, so the differentiation is very low and nevertheless statistically detectable; only the Guinea 
savanna to transitional comparison (0.00047, interval -0.00083 to 0.00178), which involves the 25-isolate 
transitional zone, spans zero.
Three things follow less readily than is often assumed. First, a very low FST is not evidence of random mating: low 
among-group variance in allele frequencies is compatible with a wide range of demographic histories, and the 
mating system is not identified by it. Second, the estimate is not an upper bound on true differentiation. The 
confidence intervals quantify sampling error around the estimate for this panel in these groups; they say nothing 
about the distance between that estimate and whatever structure exists at variants the analysis never saw. 
Third, the result is silent about the variants the minor-allele-frequency filter removed. A MAF >= 0.05 threshold 
applied to the pooled sample discards exactly the rare and geographically restricted alleles that carry recent and 
local structure, so the panel reports on common variation and only on common variation.
The three structure analyses do not independently confirm one another. The ordinations (Fig. 2; McInnes et al. 
2018), the pairwise FST estimates (Table 2) and the ADMIXTURE decomposition (Alexander et al. 2009) are 
different projections of the same 1,900 isolates: the ordinations of the published Pf8 genotype-distance 
submatrix, the FST estimates of the 3,916 retained common variants, and the decomposition of 3,385 LD-pruned 
SNPs. All three point the same way: every silhouette coefficient by zone is negative (UMAP -0.306, t-SNE -0.057, 
PCoA -0.112, original distances -0.0156), the cross-validation curve across K = 1 to 8 spans only 0.0056 with a 
shallow minimum at K = 2, and the K = 2 minor component explains eta-squared = 0.019, or 1.9% of variance, 
among zones. That concordance is coherence within one dataset, not replication across datasets.
Why the spatial signal does not survive
At the published thresholds, the Mantel test on regional FST against geographic distance returned r = 0.427, p = 
0.0252 over 21 pairs from seven administrative regions (Mantel 1967). The correlation does not hold away from 
those thresholds. Across the six sensitivity scenarios sharing one genotype load (Table 4), it is significant in two 
of six: the baseline and MAF >= 0.02 (r = 0.434, p = 0.0467). Relaxing the within-host complexity filter to Fws >= 
0.90 gives p = 0.0874, Fws >= 0.80 gives p = 0.1687, and removing the filter altogether gives p = 0.1792; MAF >= 
0.01 gives p = 0.0617. The maximum FST is stable at 0.00204 to 0.00255 across all six, so the instability is specific 
to the distance correlation, not to the differentiation estimate underlying it. Aggregating to zones rather than 
regions also removes significance (r = 0.4709, p = 0.1218).
