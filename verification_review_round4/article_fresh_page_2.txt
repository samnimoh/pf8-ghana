Malaria is endemic throughout Ghana, the entire population is at risk, and the disease remains a leading cause of 
outpatient attendance, paediatric admission and death [CITATION TO BE SUPPLIED: WHO World Malaria Report]. 
The national programme relies on artemisinin-based combination therapy for case management, sulfadoxine-
pyrimethamine for intermittent preventive treatment in pregnancy, and sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine with 
amodiaquine for seasonal malaria chemoprevention in the north. Each regimen imposes a distinct selective 
pressure on Plasmodium falciparum, and each is delivered across a country whose transmission ecology is far 
from uniform.
Ghana is divided into several agro-ecological zones, four of which are represented in this dataset; these are the 
zones that organise rainfall, vegetation and vector ecology in West Africa. From south to north these are the 
coastal savanna, the deciduous forest, the forest-savanna transitional zone and the Guinea savanna (Fig. 1). The 
coastal strip is comparatively dry; the forest belt receives bimodal rainfall and sustains intense perennial 
transmission; the transitional zone is a narrow band of mixed woodland; and the Guinea savanna of the north 
has a single wet season, so transmission there is high but sharply seasonal, which is why chemoprevention is 
targeted to it. This south-north gradient in seasonality, vector composition and mosquito survival is the natural 
axis along which Ghanaian parasite populations might be expected to differ, and it is also the axis along which 
control interventions and their selective pressures are unevenly distributed. The zones are not, however, 
independent of how Ghana is sampled: the finest spatial unit recorded in the release metadata is the 
administrative region, seven of which contribute Ghanaian isolates, and zones correspond approximately to 
groups of regions (Table 1). Zone is in consequence very nearly a relabelling of administrative region, sequencing 
study and collection period, with three of the four zones dominated by a single sequencing study, so every 
comparison drawn between zones is confounded by how the samples were gathered.
Population structure matters for resistance surveillance because surveillance is almost always local while policy 
is national. A marker frequency is measured in isolates from a handful of sentinel sites and then used to describe 
the country. The estimate generalises only if parasites are exchanged freely enough between zones that allele 
frequencies are effectively shared. If, instead, zones are differentiated, a national frequency built from 
whichever zone happens to have been sequenced most heavily is biased, and a resistance allele rising in one 
ecological setting can be diluted to invisibility in a pooled national figure. The magnitude of structure therefore 
sets the spatial resolution at which surveillance has to be designed, and it needs to be quantified with interval 
estimates rather than asserted, because very low differentiation and no differentiation have different 
operational consequences. Quantification also requires care in interpretation: an FST value close to zero describes 
how little of the variance in allele frequencies is partitioned between zones at the variants examined, and it says 
nothing about variants excluded by the frequency threshold applied.
What is known about West African P. falciparum structure provides the expectation against which Ghana must 
be assessed. Genome-wide analyses of parasites sampled across the continent resolve major sub-Saharan 
subpopulations, with West Africa forming a cluster distinct from Central, East and Southeast African parasites, 
and with relatively modest differentiation between countries inside the West African cluster (Amambua-Ngwa et 
al. 2019). Within West Africa, genome-wide scans have identified shared signals of recent selection, 
concentrated at drug-resistance loci and at antigen-encoding genes, indicating that selective pressures are 
regionally correlated even where neutral differentiation is slight (Mobegi et al. 2014). The variants used to 
monitor that pressure are catalogued and tiered by the World Health Organization, which classifies individual 
variants rather than genes and distinguishes validated from candidate markers (WHO 2025). The implication for 
a single country is that national structure is likely to be weak, and that weak structure is precisely the regime in 
which point estimates without uncertainty are least informative.
The eighth release of the MalariaGEN P. falciparum dataset, Pf8, changes what can be attempted at national 
scale. It provides uniformly called and quality-controlled genotypes for 33,325 samples at 12,493,205 variants, 
together with published per-sample resistance genotypes and genotype-distance matrices (MalariaGEN 2025).
